## How does Google decide who shows up in the map pack?

Google picks local results mainly on three things: relevance, distance and prominence. Its own help page says so, and adds that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking (Google Business Profile Help). That is good news. Budget alone can't buy a place in the map pack. The fundamentals matter.

Here is what each factor means for an owner:

- **Distance** is how far you are from the searcher, or from the place named in the search. You cannot move your office, so you win distance by being strong enough to rank farther out.
- **Prominence** is how well known you are. Reviews, ratings, links, mentions in the press and directories, and your overall website strength all feed it. Google notes that more reviews and positive ratings can help.

This is where Search Essence Optimization starts. People don't only search to find you. They search to understand you. Your profile, reviews and pages are the first answer to "who are these people and can I trust them?" The job is to make that answer clear, so the prospect and the business meet in the middle.

## What this looks like for law firms

A prospective client is deciding whether to trust you with something serious, and they now check you in more than one place. iLawyer Marketing, a legal marketing agency, surveyed 1,110 US adults and found the share using ChatGPT to research attorneys rose from 28.1% in 2025 to 41.9% in 2026, while Google fell from 86.7% to 71.9% (2026). Your profile, attorney bios and practice-area pages have to answer the same questions wherever they are read.

Ranking only matters if someone picks up. In a secret-shopper test of 500 firms, Clio found 48% could not be reached by phone, and 33% of emailed firms responded (March 24, 2025). If your map listing earns the call and nobody answers, the next firm in the pack gets the client.

Everything you publish is attorney advertising. ABA Model Rule 7.1 bars false or misleading communications. "Specialist" claims need a recognized certification, so leave out "expert" and never promise outcomes. Some states add process: the Florida Bar requires most ads be filed for review at least 20 days before first use, though websites are exempt, and Texas requires filing with its Advertising Review Committee within 10 days of first use. Ask your bar how profile posts and review replies are treated. This is not legal advice.

## What this looks like for elective practices

Elective patients research for weeks before they book, and they compare. Demand is there: the American Society of Plastic Surgeons reported cosmetic surgical volume rose 7% in 2025 over 2024, with facelifts up 13% and patients 66 and older up 24% (September 1, 2026). In dentistry the pressure runs the other way. The ADA Health Policy Institute reported capacity opening up and wait times falling (ADA News, October 1, 2025), which means more practices competing for the same implant and ortho patients.

So procedure pages need real substance. Explain who is a good candidate, what recovery looks like, honest cost ranges and financing, and who performs the procedure. A thin page that says "we offer implants" gives a careful patient nothing to understand you by.

Privacy shapes local SEO more than most owners expect. HHS guidance on tracking technologies still covers patient portals, scheduling pages and other pages that transmit protected health information, even after a federal court vacated the part covering unauthenticated public pages (June 20, 2024). Vendors that handle PHI need a business associate agreement. Keep patient information out of analytics, ad platforms, review requests and review replies. This is not legal advice.

## What this looks like for home improvement companies

The market is growing slowly, so every job is a share fight. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies projects owner-occupied improvement and repair spending moving from $509 billion in Q4 2025 to $518 billion in Q4 2026, growth of roughly 1.6% to 2.1% (April 15, 2026). When the pie barely grows, the map pack decides who gets the estimate.

Most contractors are service-area businesses. Hide your address if customers don't come to you, and list only the towns your crews actually drive to. Over-claiming a whole state doesn't widen your ranking radius, and it fills your calendar with jobs too far away to price well.

You also have something many industries don't: visible proof. Finished roofs, kitchens and HVAC installs, photographed with the homeowner's permission and tagged by town, make honest local pages easy to build. Permit notes and local weather problems give those pages real substance.

Solar has extra rules. The FTC has said sellers must disclose total cost and financing terms, must not overstate savings or tax credits, and flagged "free solar" claims as a scam signal (August 7, 2024). Keep your solar pages, profile description and posts inside those lines. This is not legal advice.

## What this looks like for senior living communities and home care agencies

The searcher is usually an adult child, and they search by the parent's town, not their own. A daughter in Columbus looking for care near her mother in Strongsville needs your profile to show up for Strongsville and your pages to explain the neighborhood, the nearby hospitals and the drive.

Occupancy is high, so families compare closely. NIC MAP reported senior housing occupancy at 89.9% in Q2 2026, with assisted living at 88.4% and independent living at 91.3% (July 20, 2026). Real photos of rooms, dining and staff carry more weight than any headline.

Owning your search visibility also reduces dependence on referral sites. Senior Housing News reported one operator received 465 referral-site leads in 2024 that produced 12 move-ins, and cancelled the contracts. Leads from your own profile come to you first. Answer them fast: Bild & Co. mystery shops found 53% of web inquiries got no response within two hours (Senior Housing News, February 12, 2024).

Home care agencies should list only the areas they can staff. Activated Insights found 63.3% of providers turned down cases in 2023 because they couldn't staff them (Home Health Care News, August 2024). A client you can't serve becomes a bad review. If you market 55+ housing, that claim depends on the Housing for Older Persons Act exemption (NAHB, November 30, 2021). This is not legal advice.

## What this looks like for recruiting and staffing firms

Your buyers are employers, and they rarely pick a staffing partner from the map pack. A hiring manager searches by specialty and city, then reads about the recruiters. So the profile still matters, but specialty-plus-city pages and recruiter bios carry most of the weight.

The market is growing, but not fast. Staffing Industry Analysts forecasts the US staffing market will grow 2.4% in 2026 to $183.1 billion (September 6, 2026). In a slow-growth market, being the clear answer for "light industrial staffing in Akron" or "accounting recruiters in Cleveland" is how a firm wins share without outspending national brands.

Your profile has two audiences. Candidates leave reviews about how they were treated. Hiring managers read those reviews to judge how you'll represent their company. Both matter, and your pages should speak to each one separately: a page for employers about how you fill roles, and a page for candidates about how you work with them.

Pages written for employers should answer the questions a buyer asks before a call: which roles you fill, in which markets, how you screen, typical time to submit candidates, and how fees and guarantees work. Plain answers to those questions are what AI assistants quote when a hiring manager asks for a recommendation.

## What this looks like for estate security firms

Your clients value discretion above almost everything, which changes how local search works for you. A service-area profile with a hidden address usually fits. Many clients will never post a public review, and you would never publish their address or a photo of their home. So prominence comes from different sources: the credentials of your principals, published explanations of your process, and mentions from architects, builders and associations you work alongside.

These are large, considered decisions. ASIS International reported that a professional threat assessment typically costs $25,000 to $50,000 (April 2025). Buyers spending at that level research carefully before they call, and they are looking for evidence of judgment, not marketing.

That makes Search Essence Optimization especially useful here. Pages that explain what an assessment includes, how a safe room project runs alongside a build, who is on site and how you protect client information let a prospect understand you before they reveal anything about themselves. Write those pages plainly and specifically, without fear-based copy.

Treat regions, not towns, as your local unit. A page for the region you serve, with how site visits and travel work, is more honest and safer than a page naming a small wealthy town.

## Why are we ranking but not getting calls?

Usually because ranking was never the real goal. Three patterns cause most of it.

First, you rank for the wrong searches. Ranking first for your own business name, or for a broad term people use to research rather than hire, looks good in a report and produces few calls.

Second, you rank in a small circle. Map rank changes block by block. You may hold the top spot near your office and fall out of the pack two miles away, where most of your buyers live.

Third, the click lands on a weak answer. A profile with eleven reviews and no photos, or a website that loads slowly on a phone, loses to a competitor one spot lower who looks more trustworthy.

There is a newer fourth cause. AI summaries now sit above many results. Pew Research Center found that Google users clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits when an AI summary appeared, compared with 15% when it didn't, and clicked a link inside the summary on 1% of visits (July 22, 2025). So pages now need to do two jobs: rank, and be written clearly enough to be quoted. Seer Interactive, an agency, found brands cited in an AI Overview earned 35% more organic clicks than brands that weren't, across 3,119 informational queries (November 4, 2025).

## Step 1: Complete your Google Business Profile

Your profile is the map pack listing. Many profiles are only half finished. Work through this list once, then review it every quarter.

- [ ] Choose the most specific **primary category** that describes your main service. This choice matters a great deal for relevance.
- [ ] Add **secondary categories** only for services you actually sell. Extra categories that don't fit can blur relevance.
- [ ] Use your real business name. Don't add keywords or towns to it. Google's Business Profile guidelines don't allow it.
- [ ] Set your address correctly, or hide it and list **service areas** if you serve clients at their location.
- [ ] Keep service areas to the region you truly serve. Listing every county in the state does not stretch your ranking radius.
- [ ] Add every **service** you offer, with a short plain description of each.
- [ ] Add **products** or service packages where the profile allows it, such as a consultation type or an inspection.
- [ ] Set regular **hours**, plus special hours for holidays.
- [ ] Note whether you take after-hours calls or emergency work, if you do.
- [ ] Write the **business description** in plain language: who you serve, what you do, where, and what makes you different. No hype.
- [ ] Upload real **photos**. Replace stock images.
- [ ] Add the attributes that apply, such as accessibility, languages spoken or free consultations.
- [ ] Answer the questions prospects ask most, in your description, services and website FAQ. Q&A features on the profile have changed over time, so put the answers where they will last.
- [ ] Add a **UTM-tagged website link** so profile visits show up separately in analytics. Example: `?utm_source=google&utm_medium=organic&utm_campaign=gbp`.
- [ ] Use **call tracking with care**. Put the tracking number as the primary phone and your main number as an additional phone, so your name, address and phone stay consistent across the web.
- [ ] Turn on **messaging** only if someone will answer within minutes during business hours.
- [ ] Add a **booking or consultation link** if you take online appointments.
- [ ] Publish a **post** at least twice a month: a recent project, a seasonal reminder, a short answer to a common question.

## Step 2: Build a review system you can defend

Reviews are the part of prominence you control most, and the part prospects read most. BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, a vendor survey of 1,002 US adults, found 97% read reviews for local businesses, 68% require at least four stars, 31% only use businesses rated 4.5 or higher, and 47% won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews (February 11, 2026).

So how many reviews do you need? There is no magic number. A useful target is to pass 20, then match the review count and recency of the three businesses in the pack for your main search. Recency matters to readers. A business with 40 reviews, the last one from two years ago, looks closed.

Keep the system honest. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, finalized in August 2024, bans fake reviews (including AI-generated ones), buying reviews, incentives conditioned on positive sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews and review suppression (Morgan Lewis, August 26, 2024). In practice:

- [ ] Ask **every** client, not only the happy ones. No "review gating" that sends unhappy clients to a private form.
- [ ] Never offer a reward tied to a positive review.
- [ ] Don't let staff or family post reviews without disclosure.
- [ ] Make the ask a set step at a set moment.
- [ ] Send a direct link by text or email, with consent, within a day of that moment.
- [ ] Reply to **every** review within a few days.
- [ ] Write each reply fresh. BrightLocal found 89% expect owners to respond to reviews and 50% are put off by templated replies.
- [ ] Respond to criticism calmly, once, and offer to talk offline.

<!-- industry:law-firms -->

For a law firm, the natural moment is when the matter closes and the client has heard the final outcome, not mid-case. In replies, never confirm that the reviewer was a client or discuss their matter, even to correct a harsh review. Thank them, keep it general and offer to talk by phone. Some state bars have rules on testimonials, so check yours before featuring reviews on your site.

In replies, never confirm the reviewer is a patient or mention any treatment, even to correct the record. A reply like "We'd welcome the chance to talk. Your profile ranks better when your website backs it up. The website needs one clear page per core service, and a page for each town you serve only when you have something real to say about that town.

Thin city pages hurt more than they help. Twenty pages that swap "Akron" for "Parma" in the same paragraph give Google nothing new and give prospects no reason to trust you. A good town page has real local substance and real local proof.

## Step 4: Run the 15-minute technical health check

Content cannot rank on a site Google cannot read. These checks use free tools and take about fifteen minutes.

- [ ] Search `site:yourdomain.com` in Google. Is the count roughly the number of real pages you have? Far fewer suggests indexing problems. Far more suggests duplicates.
- [ ] Open the **Pages** report in Google Search Console. Read the reasons pages are not indexed. "Crawled, currently not indexed" on important pages is a warning sign.
- [ ] Run your home page and top service page through **PageSpeed Insights** on the mobile tab. Note the Core Web Vitals result.
- [ ] Check **title tags** on your top ten pages. Each should be unique and name the service and place.
- [ ] Confirm each page has **one H1** that says what the page is about.
- [ ] Check **internal links**. Every service page should be reachable from the home page in two clicks, and town pages should link to their service.
- [ ] Test your **schema** with Google's Rich Results Test. At minimum: LocalBusiness or a fitting subtype, with name, address, phone and hours matching the profile.
- [ ] Confirm `robots.txt` doesn't block search or AI crawlers by accident.

Write a one-line note beside every item that fails. Those notes become your fix list.

## Step 5: Build bio pages for the people prospects search by name

Prospects often search the name of the person they'll work with before they call. If that search shows an old directory listing or nothing at all, you lose trust you already earned.

Give each key person a page with:

- [ ] Full name as it appears on licenses and directories
- [ ] Credentials, education and licenses, stated factually
- [ ] Years in practice or in the trade
- [ ] The services they personally handle
- [ ] A real photo
- [ ] Links to licensing, board or association profiles
- [ ] Person schema that links them to the business

<!-- industry:law-firms -->

For a law firm, build a page for every attorney. List each bar admission with the year, link to the state bar's public profile, and name the practice areas the attorney personally handles. Use "focuses on" rather than "specializes in" unless the attorney holds a recognized certification, and leave out "expert" and outcome claims. Bios are what both Google and AI assistants pull from when a prospect asks about a lawyer by name.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:elective-healthcare -->

For an elective practice, build a page for every surgeon, dentist or provider who sees patients. Name each board certification exactly as the certifying board does, list training and hospital affiliations, and say which procedures that person performs. Link to the state license lookup. Patients search the provider's name more than the practice's.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:home-improvement -->

For a home improvement company, start with the owner, then add project managers and crew leads who meet homeowners. Include license numbers where your state requires them, manufacturer certifications, and years in the trade. Homeowners want to know who will be in their house.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:senior-living -->

For senior living, build pages for the executive director, the director of nursing or wellness, and the person who leads tours. For home care, add the care manager who does assessments. Include licenses and years in senior care. Families want to know who is responsible for their parent's care.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:recruiting-staffing -->

For a staffing firm, build a page for each recruiter. Name the specialties and markets they cover, years recruiting, and the industries they came from, and link to their LinkedIn profiles. Employers choose a recruiter as much as a firm.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:estate-security -->

For an estate security firm, build pages for the principals and the people who lead assessments. State prior service and professional certifications factually, and describe the kind of work each person leads without naming clients. These pages are often your strongest form of prominence.

<!-- /industry -->

These pages help the person show up accurately in Google and in AI answers. They also answer the quiet question every prospect has: who will I actually be working with?

## Step 6: Measure calls and booked consultations, not rankings

Rankings are a means. Booked appointments are the result. Track what produces revenue.

<!-- industry:law-firms -->

For a law firm, the booked event is a consultation with an attorney, ideally split by practice area.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:elective-healthcare -->

For an elective practice, the booked event is a consultation, split by procedure. Count it in your practice management system, not in analytics, so no PHI leaves your systems.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:home-improvement -->

For a home improvement company, the booked event is an in-home estimate or inspection, split by trade.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:senior-living -->

For senior living, the booked event is a tour. For home care, it is an in-home assessment.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:recruiting-staffing -->

For a staffing firm, the booked event is a discovery call with a hiring manager. Keep candidate inquiries in a separate column so they don't inflate the count.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:estate-security -->

For an estate security firm, the booked event is a first meeting or assessment conversation with a principal.

Fill in this formula each month:

**Cost per booked appointment from SEO = monthly SEO spend ÷ appointments booked from search and maps.**

Example numbers, not benchmarks: $3,000 a month in SEO work that produces 15 booked appointments costs $200 per appointment. Compare that with your cost per booked appointment from bought leads, not cost per lead.

That answers "Is SEO worth it or should I buy leads?" for your business. Bought leads start fast and stop when you stop paying, and they are often shared with competitors. SEO is slower to start and keeps working while you build it. Many owners do both: paid leads now, SEO for the year after.

## Where do most owners get stuck?

> **Technical fixes on your CMS.** Knowing a page is not indexed is different from fixing the template, redirect or plugin that caused it. That work often needs a developer.

> **Seeing the whole map.** One search from your desk shows one point. Knowing where you rank across your service area takes map-grid rank data collected again and again over time.

> **Content at scale that stays compliant.** Writing dozens of service and town pages with real substance, reviewable under your field's rules, takes steady time most owners don't have.

> **Links and citations.** Earning mentions from local press, associations and directories is slow outreach work, and cleaning up old listings with the wrong phone number is tedious.

<!-- industry:law-firms -->

> **Answering the calls the map earns.** More visibility means more intake calls at odd hours. Without a tested intake process, the ranking work leaks out through voicemail.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:elective-healthcare -->

> **Tracking without PHI.** Knowing which search led to a booked consultation, without sending patient information to analytics or ad tools, takes careful setup on your scheduling pages and vendor agreements.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:home-improvement -->

> **Keeping proof current.** Town pages stay strong only if crews photograph finished jobs and someone files them by town and trade. That habit is hard to build in a busy season.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:senior-living -->

> **Turning searches into tours.** A family that finds you at 9 p.m. needs a fast, human reply. Without a follow-up process that covers evenings and weekends, the visibility goes to waste.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:recruiting-staffing -->

> **Separating the two audiences.** Candidate traffic often swamps employer traffic. Without clean tracking, it is hard to see whether search is producing client conversations or only applicants.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:estate-security -->

> **Prominence without public proof.** When clients won't leave reviews and projects can't be shown, earning trust signals through credentials, partners and published expertise takes deliberate, slow work.

<!-- /industry -->

> **Patience.** Profile fixes can move quickly. Content and links usually compound over months, and it is hard to stay the course without clear reporting tied to booked appointments.
