## Is an event right for your business?

An event is worth the effort when three things are true: the decision is large, the buyer wants to learn before they talk to anyone, and you have a person who can teach in plain language. If one of those is missing, a strong landing page and fast follow-up will usually do more for less.

That fits the idea behind Search Essence Optimization: people are not only searching to find you, they are searching to understand you. A good event lets them do that in an hour.

Run the fit test before you book a room.

- [ ] The service costs enough, or matters enough, that buyers hesitate for weeks or months.
- [ ] Buyers commonly say "I need to understand this first" or "I need to talk to my spouse."
- [ ] You have a presenter who explains things clearly and does not oversell.
- [ ] You can name one next step for attendees, such as a consultation, a home assessment or a tour.
- [ ] Someone on your team can call every attendee within a day or two of the event.

Four or more checks means an event is worth a trial. Fewer than four, fix the gaps first.

## What topic will actually fill seats?

Choose a topic phrased as the exact question your audience is already asking, not as your service name. "What happens to your house if you die without a will" fills seats. "Estate Planning Seminar" does not, because it describes what you sell rather than what they worry about.

## Should it be in person, a webinar or both?

Choose in person when trust is the main barrier and your audience is local. Choose a webinar when your audience is spread out or busy. Choose hybrid only when you have the staff to run two rooms at once.

| Factor | In person | Webinar | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Builds trust fastest | Strong | Moderate | Strong in the room, moderate online |
| Cost per attendee | Higher (venue, food, staff) | Lower | Highest to run well |
| Geographic reach | Drive time from venue | Wide | Wide |
| Best for | Older and local audiences, home and health decisions | B2B buyers, busy professionals, wider service areas | Established series with a steady crowd |
| Biggest risk | Empty seats you already paid for | Low live attendance, multitasking | Neither audience gets full attention |
| Replay value | Needs recording setup | Built in | Built in |

Whatever you choose, judge it by cost per booked consultation, not cost per registration. A cheap webinar that books no one is more expensive than a full room that books six.

## What does the registration page need?

A registration page has one job: get the right person to sign up in under a minute. Everything on it should serve that.

- [ ] A headline that repeats the question from your topic
- [ ] Date, time, time zone and location or "online" above the form
- [ ] Three bullets on what attendees will learn
- [ ] Who is presenting, with a short, factual bio
- [ ] A short form: name, email, phone and a text-consent checkbox that is not pre-checked
- [ ] Clear consent language next to the checkbox, stating they agree to receive event texts and can reply STOP
- [ ] An "add to calendar" link on the thank-you page and in the confirmation email
- [ ] Parking, accessibility and guest details for in-person events
- [ ] A plain statement that the event is educational and attendees will not be pressured

## When should reminders go out?

Send five reminders: at confirmation, one week before, one day before, the morning of, and 15 minutes before. ON24's November 26, 2025 advice covers the one-week, one-day and 15-minute reminders; the confirmation and morning-of messages are additions we recommend for local events.

| Reminder | Email | Text (only with written consent) | What it says |
|---|---|---|---|
| Confirmation | Yes | Yes | You are registered, date, time, calendar link |
| One week before | Yes | Optional | One thing they will learn, reply if plans change |
| One day before | Yes | Yes | Time, location or link, parking or tech note |
| Morning of | Optional | Yes | "See you at 6:30," with the address or link |
| 15 minutes before | Optional | Yes | The link or the door to use |

Texts need care. Under the TCPA, prior express written consent is still required for autodialed or prerecorded marketing texts. Since April 11, 2025, people can revoke consent by any reasonable means, such as replying STOP or QUIT, and opt-outs must be honored within 10 business days (Kelley Drye; Burr & Forman). Honor STOP immediately and keep a record of who opted in and when.

Email has rules too. The FTC's CAN-SPAM guide requires a postal address and an opt-out honored within 10 business days. Google's sender guidelines (February 1, 2024) require bulk Gmail senders to use SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

## How many registrations do you actually need?

Work backward from the seats you want filled. Many owners plan from the top of the funnel and end up guessing. Start at the room instead.

**Formula:**

Seats wanted ÷ show rate = registrations needed
Registrations needed ÷ registration-page conversion rate = clicks needed
Clicks needed × cost per click = estimated ad budget

**Example numbers, not benchmarks.** You want 30 people in the room. If 50% of registrants show, you need 60 registrations. If 15% of page visitors register, you need 400 clicks. At $2.50 per click, that is about $1,000 in ads.

For reference, ON24 reported an average registrant-to-attendee rate of 60% in its 2025 data (June 19, 2026). That figure comes from a vendor and mostly reflects B2B enterprise webinars, so treat it as context, not a target for a local seminar.

## How should the presentation work?

Teach first, offer one clear next step, and apply no pressure. People came to learn. If they leave feeling sold to, they will not book, and they will tell their friends.

- [ ] Open with the question in the title and answer it within the first ten minutes.
- [ ] Leave 15 minutes for questions. Questions show you what to cover next time.
- [ ] Name one next step, such as a 30-minute consultation or a free home assessment, and explain exactly what happens in it.
- [ ] Hand out a one-page summary with a way to book.
- [ ] Have a short feedback card or poll: "Would you like someone to call you?" with a yes or no.

The feedback card matters. It tells your team who wants a call and who wants space.

## What should happen in the 72 hours after the event?

Follow up the same day and call every attendee within 72 hours. Interest fades quickly. In Harvard Business Review research on online leads (March 2011), firms that contacted leads within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify them as firms that waited longer. An attendee is warmer than a web lead, but the same clock applies.

| When | Attendees | Registered no-shows |
|---|---|---|
| Same day | Thank-you email with the summary and a booking link | "Sorry we missed you" email with the replay or next date |
| Day 1 | Call those who said yes on the feedback card | Text (with consent) offering the replay |
| Day 2 | Call remaining attendees once; leave a short voicemail | Second email with one key point from the talk |
| Day 3 | Email anyone not reached with a direct booking link | Invite to the next event |

Track every attendee in one list: name, attended (Y/N), wants a call (Y/N), called (date), booked (date), became a client (date).

## What compliance items apply?

Check these before launch. This is general information, not legal advice; confirm specifics with your counsel.

- **Lawyer advertising.** The Florida Bar requires most ads to be filed for review at least 20 days before first use; websites are exempt. Texas requires filing with the Advertising Review Committee within 10 days of first use. ABA Model Rule 7.1 bars misleading communications, and Rule 7.3 limits live person-to-person solicitation, so keep after-event outreach to people who asked for it.
- **No outcome promises.** No guaranteed results in any industry. Lawyers should avoid "specialist" or "expert" claims unless certified.
- **Health claims.** Keep claims about procedures factual. HHS guidance still covers tracking on scheduling and other pages that transmit protected health information, and vendors handling it need a business associate agreement. Keep health details out of ad platforms and texts.
- **Texting.** Get written consent, honor STOP, keep records (TCPA, above).

## What changes in your industry?

### Law firms

Estate planning and family law seminars fit well because the questions are personal and people want to learn privately first. File seminar ads where your state requires it, and avoid any statement that implies a result. After the event, contact only attendees who asked for follow-up.

### Elective healthcare

Implant, LASIK and cosmetic seminars let patients see the surgeon before they commit to a consultation. Search Engine Land reported on March 11, 2025 that Meta's restrictions on health advertisers can block optimizing toward scheduling events, with in-app lead forms affected least. Keep reminder texts generic, such as "your Thursday seminar," with no procedure details.

### Home improvement

Storm, solar and remodel seminars work when they answer a real cost question. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies projected owner-occupied improvement and repair spending growth to peak around 2.1% in mid-2026 and ease to 1.6% by year end (April 15, 2026), so homeowners will compare carefully. For solar topics, the FTC (August 7, 2024) expects total cost and financing terms disclosed and no overstated savings or tax credits.

### Senior living and home care

Family information sessions work because the decision is often made by adult children on a deadline. Hold them in the community so the event doubles as a tour. Community ads can fall under Meta's housing Special Ad Category, which restricts age and ZIP targeting (Meta Marketing API docs). Invite local care managers to share the invitation.

### Recruiting and staffing

Employer-side webinars on hiring outlooks fit busy plant and operations managers who will not drive to a seminar. Staffing Industry Analysts forecast the US staffing market to grow 2.4% in 2026 to $183.1 billion (September 6, 2026), so a market-outlook talk has fresh material. CAN-SPAM applies to B2B email, so keep the opt-out working.

### Estate security

Small, invitation-only briefings suit this audience better than open seminars. Privacy matters to them, so collect only what you need on registration. ASIS International noted in April 2025 that a professional threat assessment typically costs $25,000 to $50,000, which shows how much trust a buyer needs before booking.

## Where do most owners get stuck?

> **Filling seats at a sane cost.** The first event teaches you what your cost per registration really is. Getting it down takes testing topics, audiences and ad creative across several events, and reading the numbers before money runs out.

> **Reminder automation across email and text.** Building a five-touch sequence that pulls from your registration form, respects text consent, honors STOP and stops sending to people who cancel takes systems work most teams do not have time for.

> **Attribution from seat to signed client.** Most owners know how many people registered. Few can tell which ad, topic or event produced the clients who signed three months later. That needs one connected record from click to close.

> **Running it every month without burning out the presenter.** One good event is easy to plan. A monthly series needs a topic calendar, reusable slides, a stable team for follow-up calls and a way to keep the presenter fresh.
