Many owners who say Facebook ads "don't work" are judging them on the wrong number. They see a cheap lead, call it, get voicemail, and decide the channel is junk. Or they see an expensive lead and turn the campaign off before it has a chance to book anyone.

This guide gives you a different scoreboard. You will set the most you can pay for a booked appointment, then build everything else around hitting it. Clients search to understand you before they ever talk to you. A good Meta campaign meets them in the middle with an offer that makes sense for where they are.

## Step 1: What can you afford to pay for a booked appointment?

Your ceiling comes from your own numbers, not from an industry average. Fill in the formula below with the last 12 months of your data. If you don't track a number yet, use your honest best guess and mark it as a guess.

**The formula**

1. Average first-year value of a new client × gross margin = **value of a new client**
2. Value of a new client × close rate from a held appointment = **value of a held appointment**
3. Value of a held appointment × show rate = **value of a booked appointment**
4. Value of a booked appointment × the share you're willing to spend on acquisition = **your maximum cost per booked appointment**

Use first-year value, not lifetime value, for this ceiling. It keeps you safe while you learn what Meta leads actually do.

**Your worksheet**

| Line | Your number |
|---|---|
| Average first-year value of a new client | $ |
| Gross margin (%) | |
| Value of a new client | $ |
| Close rate from a held appointment (%) | |
| Value of a held appointment | $ |
| Show rate for booked appointments (%) | |
| Value of a booked appointment | $ |
| Share you'll spend on acquisition (%) | |
| **Maximum cost per booked appointment** | **$** |

**A worked example.** Example numbers, not benchmarks.

- First-year value $8,000 × 50% margin = $4,000 per new client
- $4,000 × 25% close rate = $1,000 per held appointment
- $1,000 × 70% show rate = $700 per booked appointment
- $700 × 30% spend share = **$210 maximum cost per booked appointment**

Now work backward. If 40% of this business's leads book an appointment, it can pay up to $84 per lead and still hit its target. If only 10% book, the same campaign needs leads under $21. That's why cost per lead alone tells you so little. A $30 lead that books half the time is cheaper than a $12 lead that almost never does.

## What this looks like for law firms

For a law firm, a bad Meta campaign costs more than wasted spend. It costs a paralegal's afternoon calling people who tapped a form by accident, while someone with a real matter waits for a callback. That's why the ceiling from Step 1 matters. It tells you how much a booked consultation is worth before you spend a dollar.

Prospects also research you before they ever answer your call. In a 2026 consumer survey of 1,110 US adults by iLawyer Marketing, a legal marketing agency, the share using ChatGPT to research attorneys rose from 28.1% to 41.9% in a year. Someone who sees your ad will likely look you up first. Your ad, your site and your reviews need to tell the same plain story about who you help and how.

Your offers have to stay inside the advertising rules. That means no promised results, no "specialist" or "expert" label unless the lawyer holds a recognized certification, and no wording that implies an outcome. Some states require ads to be filed for review. Texas extended its barratry law to social media and electronic messages (Texas HB 2733, effective September 1, 2025), so treat every follow-up text with the same care as the ad.

Keep case facts off the form. A free case review or a practical checklist gets the hand raised. The facts come out on the call, where a person can handle them properly.

## What this looks like for elective practices

Elective patients are interested, but rarely in a hurry. Someone considering implants, a facelift or LASIK may watch your videos for months before raising a hand. A Meta campaign works when it gives that person a low-pressure next step, and someone answers quickly when they take it.

Demand is there. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons reported that cosmetic surgical procedure volume rose 7% in 2025, with facelifts up 13% and patients 66 and older up 24% (ASPS, September 1, 2026). On the dental side, the ADA Health Policy Institute reported that capacity is opening up and appointment wait times are falling (ADA News, October 1, 2025). More open chair time makes every unbooked lead more expensive.

Meta makes this harder than it used to be. Its 2025 restrictions on health and wellness advertisers can block optimization toward lower-funnel events such as scheduling, while in-app lead forms were affected least (Search Engine Land, March 11, 2025). That makes Instant Forms a practical starting point for most practices.

Privacy is the line you don't cross. Ask about timing and interest, never about conditions or treatment history. Keep anything that could be health information out of Meta, out of your analytics and out of text messages. Your ads should speak to what the person wants to learn, and never imply you know anything about them.

## What this looks like for home improvement companies

Home improvement leads go cold fast. A homeowner with a leaking roof or a dead furnace often requests several quotes in one evening, and the first company to call back with a real person gets the first conversation. A well-built campaign can still lose if the phone sits until morning.

The market is steady, not booming. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies projects owner-occupied improvement and repair spending to grow from $509 billion in late 2025 to $518 billion by late 2026, growth of roughly 1.6–2.1% (Harvard JCHS, April 15, 2026). In a flat market, you win by converting more of the leads you already pay for.

A free estimate or inspection is the natural offer. A location question on the form saves your crews wasted drives, and a project-type question routes roofing, HVAC and window leads to the right person.

Solar needs extra care. The FTC says solar sellers must disclose total cost and financing terms, must not overstate savings or tax credits, and flags "free solar" claims as a scam warning sign (FTC, August 7, 2024). Any ad that leads with financing deserves the same caution, and it may fall under Meta's credit category, which restricts targeting. Say what the work involves and how payment works. Let the estimate do the selling.

## What this looks like for senior living communities and home care agencies

The person filling out your form is often not the future resident or client. It's an adult child researching for a parent, between work and their own family. Your ad, form and first call need to speak to that person: what the next step is, what it involves, and that a real human will help.

Occupancy is high, which changes the job. NIC MAP reported senior housing occupancy at 89.9% in Q2 2026, with assisted living at 88.4% and independent living at 91.3% (NIC MAP, July 20, 2026). Communities near full need fewer, better-fit leads. Communities with openings usually need steadier follow-up more than more volume.

Follow-up is long. Aline, a senior living CRM vendor, reported that web leads often need three to four touches to get a response, and that a move-in takes about 28 sales activities (Aline 2024 Benchmark Report, March 4, 2025). Plan your team's time for that before you raise the budget.

Meta targeting is limited. Senior housing ads can fall under the housing Special Ad Category, so the message has to do the targeting. Home care agencies face a different constraint. Activated Insights reported caregiver turnover of 79.2%, and 63.3% of providers turned down cases in 2023 because they couldn't staff them (via Home Health Care News, August 2024). Don't buy leads you can't serve.

A tour, or a care needs conversation, usually makes a better offer than a brochure.

## What this looks like for recruiting and staffing firms

Your buyer is an employer, not a job seeker, and that changes how a Meta campaign should run. Owners and hiring managers scroll Facebook and Instagram on their own time. Your ad has to catch a business problem in a personal moment: a role that has stayed open too long, a team stretched thin, a busy season coming.

Growth in the market is modest. Staffing Industry Analysts expects the US staffing market to grow 2.4% in 2026, to $183.1 billion (SIA, September 6, 2026). In a slow market, new accounts go to firms that follow up well, not the ones that spend the most.

Keep employer campaigns and job ads apart. Ads that sell your service to employers generally don't fall under Meta's employment Special Ad Category. Job ads do, with restricted targeting and no lookalike audiences. Mixing them in one campaign muddies your data and invites review problems.

Qualify on company size, the roles they hire for and their hiring timeline. A hiring needs call is the most direct offer. A salary guide for one role family suits buyers who are still planning.

Automation helps, but not on the first call. Bullhorn, a staffing software vendor, found in its GRID 2025 survey of more than 1,500 recruitment professionals that firms focused on full-cycle automation in 2024 were more than twice as likely to see revenue growth (via SIA, March 25, 2025). That is a vendor survey and a correlation. Automate the admin. Keep the first conversation human.

## What this looks like for estate security firms

Your prospects value privacy above almost everything, and that shapes every part of the campaign. Volume will be low. A good month may bring only a handful of serious inquiries, so every lead deserves a fast, careful response, and the path to a first meeting is longer.

The stakes are high on both sides. ASIS International notes that a professional threat assessment typically costs $25,000 to $50,000 (ASIS, April 2025). Prospects who are weighing that kind of decision expect a confidential first step, not a hard sell.

Use the Higher intent form or a short landing page, and ask for as little as possible. Don't ask about the property, the family, travel schedules or existing systems in an ad platform's form. Your ad should never name a neighborhood or suggest you know who is looking at it. The real conversation belongs on a private call.

Restraint applies to creative, too. We favor calm, specific explanations of what a safe room or a security walkthrough actually involves over fear-based hooks. The people you want to reach are already thinking about risk. They're searching to understand whether you are discreet and competent. Your campaign should show them both before the first call.

## Step 2: Which offer is worth raising a hand for?

Pick one offer per campaign. The offer is the reason a stranger stops scrolling and gives you their phone number. It should match how ready they are, and it should never promise a result.

There are five offer types that tend to fit high-value services:

- **Consultation.** A conversation with the person who will do the work. Strong for ready buyers.
- **Estimate or quote.** A specific price for a specific job. Strong when the need is urgent and concrete.
- **Guide.** A useful document for people still researching. More leads, slower to book.
- **Event.** A seminar, open house or webinar. Good for considered decisions and building trust before a meeting.
- **Assessment.** A structured review of someone's situation. Feels more valuable than a "free consultation" if it is real.

## Step 3: Lead form or landing page?

Start with Meta's Instant Form set to Higher intent, add one or two qualifying questions, and move to a landing page only if lead quality stays low. That order keeps friction low while you learn.

Meta's Instant Forms come in two main types. **More volume** is quick to submit, with fields pre-filled from the person's profile. **Higher intent** adds a review screen where the person confirms their details before submitting. That extra step costs some volume. It can also screen out some accidental taps.

| Option | Use it when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Instant Form, More volume | You need data fast and your team can call everyone quickly | Accidental submissions, wrong numbers |
| Instant Form, Higher intent | You want fewer, more deliberate leads | Slightly higher cost per lead |
| Instant Form with qualifying questions | You need to route or screen leads (location, timeline, type of case or project) | Too many questions kill completion |
| Landing page | Your offer needs explanation, or you want booking on the spot | Needs fast load, clean tracking and a short form |

Good qualifying questions are easy to answer and useful to your team. Ask about timeline, location or the type of service. Use multiple choice, not open text. Don't ask anything you wouldn't need for the first call.

Keep sensitive details off the form.

## Step 4: What are the rules for your category?

Some industries run under special rules on Meta, and getting this wrong can get ads rejected or accounts restricted. Check your category before you build. This is a factual summary, not legal advice.

<!-- industry:law-firms -->

- **Lawyer advertising.** Ads must not be false or misleading (ABA Model Rule 7.1). Specialist claims need certification. Rule 7.3 limits live person-to-person solicitation.
- **Filing for review.** Some states require filing ads. The Florida Bar requires most ads be filed at least 20 days before first use (websites are exempt). Texas requires filing with its Advertising Review Committee within 10 days of first use.
- **Follow-up messages.** Texas HB 2733, effective September 1, 2025, extends the state's barratry law to social media and electronic messages, which matters for how you follow up.
- **AI intake.** Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1 (January 2024) says AI intake chatbots should disclose they are AI and not give legal advice (Hinshaw, February 5, 2024).

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:elective-healthcare -->

- **Health and wellness.** Meta's 2025 restrictions on health and wellness advertisers can block optimization toward lower-funnel events such as scheduling. In-app lead forms were affected least (Search Engine Land, March 11, 2025).
- **Tracking and HIPAA.** HHS says tracking on patient portals, scheduling pages and other pages that transmit protected health information is still covered by HIPAA, and vendors that handle it need a business associate agreement (HHS OCR guidance). A 2024 court ruling vacated the part of the guidance covering unauthenticated public pages, but your booking flow is not one of those.

If it does, age targeting is fixed at 18–65+, gender and ZIP code targeting are restricted, and lookalike audiences are unavailable (Meta Marketing API documentation).
- **Solar claims.** Solar sellers must disclose total cost and financing terms and must not overstate savings or tax credits (FTC, August 7, 2024).

- **Employment.** If you also advertise caregiver or staff jobs, those ads fall under the employment category. Keep them in separate campaigns.

## Step 5: How do you test creative in 14 days?

Run three hooks across two formats for 14 days, change only one thing at a time, and judge the winners on booked appointments. The hook, meaning the first line or first two seconds, is usually the biggest lever you control.

**The test grid**

| | Format A: short video (talking to camera) | Format B: single image with text |
|---|---|---|
| Hook 1: the problem in the prospect's words | | |
| Hook 2: what happens at the appointment | | |
| Hook 3: a common mistake or myth | | |

Six ads, one offer, one audience, one form. Keep everything else the same, so when one ad pulls ahead, you know why.

**The 14-day plan**

- [ ] Days 1–3: Launch all six. Don't touch anything. Meta needs time to spread spend.
- [ ] Days 4–7: Check which hooks get leads at all. Don't pause anything yet unless an ad has spent well past your target cost per booked appointment with zero leads.
- [ ] Days 8–14: Look at booked appointments by ad. Pause the clear losers. Keep the top one or two.
- [ ] Day 15: Write down what you learned. Build the next round by changing one thing, such as a new hook or a new format, while keeping the winner running.

**What to watch, in order of importance**

1. Booked appointments and cost per booked appointment
2. Contact rate (how many leads you actually reached)
3. Cost per lead
4. Click-through rate and hook rate (how many people watched past the first seconds)

The bottom two help you diagnose. The top two decide.

## Step 6: What happens after someone fills out the form?

Call every lead within minutes, track each one through to a result, and feed those results back to Meta when you can do it safely. This step decides whether your campaign works more than any ad does.

Speed matters. In a 2011 Harvard Business Review audit of 2,241 US companies, only 37% responded to a web lead within an hour, and 23% never responded. In a separate data set of 1.25 million leads, firms that made contact within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that waited longer (HBR, March 2011).

## Where do most owners get stuck?

> **Sending CRM outcomes back to Meta correctly.** Meta's Conversions API can receive events such as "booked" or "held" from your own system, but the setup has to match lead IDs, send the right event at the right time, and leave out anything sensitive. Getting it wrong teaches Meta the wrong lesson.

> **Making enough creative to beat fatigue.** A winning ad wears out. Staying ahead means producing new hooks and formats every few weeks, testing them cleanly and retiring the old ones. Most small teams run out of time or ideas before the campaign runs out of steam.

> **Separating junk leads from real ones.** Some bad leads are fake numbers or accidental taps. Others are real people who weren't reached fast enough. Telling the two apart takes call notes, consistent CRM fields and someone who reviews them every week.

> **Reading results with small numbers.** Ten booked appointments can look like a trend when it's noise. Knowing when to wait, when to cut and when to change the offer instead of the ad comes from seeing many campaigns, not one.

<!-- industry:law-firms -->

> **Keeping every ad reviewable.** Each new hook is a new ad that may need to be checked or filed under your state's rules. Tracking versions, approvals and filing dates while testing every few weeks is real work that often falls on the attorney.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:elective-healthcare -->

> **Measuring without exposing patient data.** Linking a Meta lead to a held consultation without sending health information anywhere it shouldn't go takes careful setup, a business associate agreement where needed, and someone who checks it after every website change.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:home-improvement -->

> **Covering the phone after hours.** Homeowners fill out forms at night and on weekends. Getting a real person on the line within minutes, when your office is closed and your crews are on roofs, is a staffing and routing problem before it's a marketing one.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:senior-living -->

> **Sustaining a long follow-up.** A family may take weeks or months to decide. Keeping 28 touches personal and on schedule, without the family feeling chased, takes a clear cadence, a CRM that reminds people, and someone who owns it.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:recruiting-staffing -->

> **Reaching the person who decides.** Meta can bring in office managers, job seekers and owners in the same week. Sorting them fast, and getting the hiring decision-maker on a call, takes qualifying questions and routing that most firms build by trial and error.

<!-- /industry -->

<!-- industry:estate-security -->

> **Keeping lead data private.** A list of people asking about safe rooms is sensitive in itself. Deciding who can see it, where it's stored and what gets sent back to ad platforms is a setup question most firms haven't answered yet.
