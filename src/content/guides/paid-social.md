Many owners who say Facebook ads "don't work" are judging them on the wrong number. They see a cheap lead, call it, get voicemail, and decide the channel is junk. Or they see an expensive lead and turn the campaign off before it has a chance to book anyone.

This guide gives you a different scoreboard. You will set the most you can pay for a booked appointment, then build everything else around hitting it. Clients search to understand you before they ever talk to you. A good Meta campaign meets them in the middle with an offer that makes sense for where they are.

## Step 1: What can you afford to pay for a booked appointment?

Your ceiling comes from your own numbers, not from an industry average. Fill in the formula below with the last 12 months of your data. If you don't track a number yet, use your honest best guess and mark it as a guess.

**The formula**

1. Average first-year value of a new client × gross margin = **value of a new client**
2. Value of a new client × close rate from a held appointment = **value of a held appointment**
3. Value of a held appointment × show rate = **value of a booked appointment**
4. Value of a booked appointment × the share you're willing to spend on acquisition = **your maximum cost per booked appointment**

Use first-year value, not lifetime value, for this ceiling. It keeps you safe while you learn what Meta leads actually do.

**Your worksheet**

| Line | Your number |
|---|---|
| Average first-year value of a new client | $ |
| Gross margin (%) | |
| Value of a new client | $ |
| Close rate from a held appointment (%) | |
| Value of a held appointment | $ |
| Show rate for booked appointments (%) | |
| Value of a booked appointment | $ |
| Share you'll spend on acquisition (%) | |
| **Maximum cost per booked appointment** | **$** |

**A worked example.** Example numbers, not benchmarks.

- First-year value $8,000 × 50% margin = $4,000 per new client
- $4,000 × 25% close rate = $1,000 per held appointment
- $1,000 × 70% show rate = $700 per booked appointment
- $700 × 30% spend share = **$210 maximum cost per booked appointment**

Now work backward. If 40% of this business's leads book an appointment, it can pay up to $84 per lead and still hit its target. If only 10% book, the same campaign needs leads under $21. That's why cost per lead alone tells you so little. A $30 lead that books half the time is cheaper than a $12 lead that almost never does.

- [ ] Write your maximum cost per booked appointment somewhere your team can see it

## Step 2: Which offer is worth raising a hand for?

Pick one offer per campaign. The offer is the reason a stranger stops scrolling and gives you their phone number. It should match how ready they are, and it should never promise a result.

There are five offer types that tend to fit high-value services:

- **Consultation.** A conversation with the person who will do the work. Strong for ready buyers.
- **Estimate or quote.** A specific price for a specific job. Strong when the need is urgent and concrete.
- **Guide.** A useful document for people still researching. More leads, slower to book.
- **Event.** A seminar, open house or webinar. Good for considered decisions and building trust before a meeting.
- **Assessment.** A structured review of someone's situation. Feels more valuable than a "free consultation" if it is real.

**Offer bank by industry**

| Industry | Offer ideas |
|---|---|
| Law firms | Free case review, estate planning workshop, "what to do after an accident" checklist |
| Elective healthcare | Consultation with the provider, candidacy questionnaire, open house or seminar |
| Home improvement | Free on-site estimate, roof or window inspection, financing explainer |
| Senior living and home care | Tour and lunch, care needs conversation, family guide to paying for care |
| Recruiting and staffing | Hiring needs call, salary guide for one role family, workforce planning session |
| Estate security | Private risk conversation, home security walkthrough, safe room planning guide |

Test your offer against three questions.

- [ ] Would a serious prospect find this useful even if they never hire us?
- [ ] Can we deliver it well, every time, at the volume a campaign could send?
- [ ] Does the wording avoid any promised outcome, savings figure or result?

## Step 3: Lead form or landing page?

Start with Meta's Instant Form set to Higher intent, add one or two qualifying questions, and move to a landing page only if lead quality stays low. That order keeps friction low while you learn.

Meta's Instant Forms come in two main types. **More volume** is quick to submit, with fields pre-filled from the person's profile. **Higher intent** adds a review screen where the person confirms their details before submitting. That extra step costs some volume. It can also screen out some accidental taps.

| Option | Use it when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Instant Form, More volume | You need data fast and your team can call everyone quickly | Accidental submissions, wrong numbers |
| Instant Form, Higher intent | You want fewer, more deliberate leads | Slightly higher cost per lead |
| Instant Form with qualifying questions | You need to route or screen leads (location, timeline, type of case or project) | Too many questions kill completion |
| Landing page | Your offer needs explanation, or you want booking on the spot | Needs fast load, clean tracking and a short form |

Good qualifying questions are easy to answer and useful to your team. Ask about timeline, location or the type of service. Use multiple choice, not open text. Don't ask anything you wouldn't need for the first call.

Keep sensitive details off the form. A healthcare practice should never ask about a diagnosis or condition in an ad platform's form. A law firm should not ask for the facts of a case there.

## Step 4: What are the rules for your category?

Some industries run under special rules on Meta, and getting this wrong can get ads rejected or accounts restricted. Check your category before you build. This is a factual summary, not legal advice.

- **Housing, employment and credit.** Meta's Special Ad Categories include housing, employment and credit. For these, age targeting is fixed at 18–65+, gender and ZIP code targeting are restricted, and lookalike audiences are unavailable (Meta Marketing API documentation). Senior housing ads can fall under the housing category. Claims about 55+ communities depend on the Housing for Older Persons Act exemption (NAHB, November 30, 2021).
- **Employment.** Ads for jobs fall under the employment category. Ads that sell a staffing service to employers generally don't, but check each campaign.
- **Health and wellness.** Meta's 2025 restrictions on health and wellness advertisers can block optimization toward lower-funnel events such as scheduling. In-app lead forms were affected least (Search Engine Land, March 11, 2025). Separately, HHS says tracking on patient portals, scheduling pages and other pages that transmit protected health information is still covered by HIPAA, and vendors that handle it need a business associate agreement (HHS OCR guidance).
- **Lawyers.** Ads must not be false or misleading (ABA Model Rule 7.1), and specialist claims need certification. Some states require filing ads for review; the Florida Bar, for example, requires most ads be filed at least 20 days before first use. Texas HB 2733, effective September 1, 2025, extends the state's barratry law to social media and electronic messages, which matters for how you follow up.
- **Texts and calls.** Prior express written consent is still required for autodialed or prerecorded marketing calls and texts. Since April 11, 2025, consumers can revoke consent by any reasonable means, such as replying STOP, and opt-outs must be honored within 10 business days (Kelley Drye; Burr & Forman).
- **Testimonials.** The FTC's final rule on reviews and testimonials, issued in August 2024, bans fake reviews, buying reviews and undisclosed insider reviews (Morgan Lewis, August 26, 2024).

- [ ] Confirm whether your campaign needs a Special Ad Category
- [ ] Remove any targeting the category doesn't allow
- [ ] Check ad copy against your profession's advertising rules
- [ ] Confirm your consent language before anyone texts a lead

## Step 5: How do you test creative in 14 days?

Run three hooks across two formats for 14 days, change only one thing at a time, and judge the winners on booked appointments. The hook, meaning the first line or first two seconds, is usually the biggest lever you control.

**The test grid**

| | Format A: short video (talking to camera) | Format B: single image with text |
|---|---|---|
| Hook 1: the problem in the prospect's words | | |
| Hook 2: what happens at the appointment | | |
| Hook 3: a common mistake or myth | | |

Six ads, one offer, one audience, one form. Keep everything else the same, so when one ad pulls ahead, you know why.

**The 14-day plan**

- [ ] Days 1–3: Launch all six. Don't touch anything. Meta needs time to spread spend.
- [ ] Days 4–7: Check which hooks get leads at all. Don't pause anything yet unless an ad has spent well past your target cost per booked appointment with zero leads.
- [ ] Days 8–14: Look at booked appointments by ad. Pause the clear losers. Keep the top one or two.
- [ ] Day 15: Write down what you learned. Build the next round by changing one thing, such as a new hook or a new format, while keeping the winner running.

**What to watch, in order of importance**

1. Booked appointments and cost per booked appointment
2. Contact rate (how many leads you actually reached)
3. Cost per lead
4. Click-through rate and hook rate (how many people watched past the first seconds)

The bottom two help you diagnose. The top two decide.

## Step 6: What happens after someone fills out the form?

Call every lead within minutes, track each one through to a result, and feed those results back to Meta when you can do it safely. This step decides whether your campaign works more than any ad does.

Speed matters. In a 2011 Harvard Business Review audit of 2,241 US companies, only 37% responded to a web lead within an hour, and 23% never responded. In a separate data set of 1.25 million leads, firms that made contact within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that waited longer (HBR, March 2011). The same problem shows up by industry. In a 2023 mystery shop of senior living communities, 53% of web inquiries got no response within two hours (Bild & Co. via Senior Housing News, February 12, 2024). In a Clio secret-shopper test of 500 UK law firms, 48% couldn't be reached by phone (Clio UK, March 24, 2025).

A human voice on the first call matters too. Automation can send the confirmation text. A person should make the call.

Once you have this data, you can send outcomes back to Meta so it learns which leads became appointments. For healthcare businesses, this must never include health information, and in many cases it shouldn't happen at all without a careful review.

- [ ] Add an "ad or hook" field to every lead record
- [ ] Review the table weekly against your maximum cost per booked appointment

## What changes in your industry?

### Law firms

Lead with a case review or a practical checklist, never a promised result or a "specialist" claim unless the lawyer is certified. Some states require ad filing, and Texas extended its barratry law to social media and electronic messages, so follow-up texts need the same care as the ad. Keep case facts off the Instant Form and gather them on the call.

### Elective healthcare

Expect Meta's health and wellness restrictions to limit optimization toward scheduling events, which makes in-app lead forms a practical starting point. Ask only about timing and interest, never about conditions. Keep any data that could be health information out of Meta, your analytics and your text messages.

### Home improvement

A free estimate or inspection is a natural offer, and a location question on the form saves your team wasted trips. Solar companies need extra care: the FTC says solar sellers must disclose total cost and financing terms and must not overstate savings or tax credits (FTC, August 7, 2024). Speed matters most here, because homeowners often request several quotes at once.

### Senior living and home care

Senior housing ads can fall under Meta's housing Special Ad Category, which fixes age targeting at 18–65+ and removes lookalikes, so the message has to do the targeting. Adult children often research for a parent, so speak to them. A tour or a care needs conversation is usually a better offer than a brochure.

### Recruiting and staffing

Ads selling your service to employers generally don't fall under the employment category, but job ads do, so keep those campaigns separate. A hiring needs call or a salary guide for one role family works well for employer-side lead generation. Qualify on company size and hiring timeline.

### Estate security

Volume will be low and every lead matters, so use the Higher intent form or a short landing page and expect a longer path to a meeting. Discretion is part of the offer. Ask for as little as possible on the form and move the real conversation to a private call.

## Where do most owners get stuck?

> **Sending CRM outcomes back to Meta correctly.** Meta's Conversions API can receive events such as "booked" or "held" from your own system, but the setup has to match lead IDs, send the right event at the right time, and leave out anything sensitive. Getting it wrong teaches Meta the wrong lesson, and for healthcare it can create real compliance exposure.

> **Making enough creative to beat fatigue.** A winning ad wears out. Staying ahead means producing new hooks and formats every few weeks, testing them cleanly and retiring the old ones. Most small teams run out of time or ideas before the campaign runs out of steam.

> **Separating junk leads from real ones.** Some bad leads are fake numbers or accidental taps. Others are real people who weren't reached fast enough. Telling the two apart takes call notes, consistent CRM fields and someone who reviews them every week.

> **Reading results with small numbers.** Ten booked appointments can look like a trend when it's noise. Knowing when to wait, when to cut and when to change the offer instead of the ad comes from seeing many campaigns, not one.
