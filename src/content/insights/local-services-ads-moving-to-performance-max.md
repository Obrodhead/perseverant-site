If you run Local Services Ads (the pay-per-lead ads at the top of Google with the Google Verified badge), the dashboard you know is going away. Google is folding those campaigns into Google Ads as a special version of Performance Max.

On paper, little changes. You still pay per lead. In practice, you're handing Google more of the decisions and keeping less of your own history. That's worth a clear look before your migration email arrives.

## What exactly is changing?

The short version: same ads, new home, less control. Here's what Google's help article on the transition says.

- **The old dashboard closes.** After your account migrates, logging in to the Local Services Ads dashboard sends you to Google Ads instead.
- **Manual bidding ends.** You can no longer set a maximum cost per lead. Separate cost targets for each service go away too. Google calculates one target cost per lead for the whole campaign.
- **Budgets turn daily.** Your weekly budget becomes a daily average, the weekly amount divided by seven. Monthly spend is capped at that daily average times 30.4, and individual days can run higher or lower.
- **Old reports don't come with you.** Historical spend, clicks, impressions and weekly spend don't transfer. Past leads, messages and call recordings do.
- **Your Google Business Profile drives your ad.** Business name, address and hours sync from your profile. A significant change to your name, address or main category triggers a 24 to 48 hour review, and your campaign may pause during it.
- **BBB callouts are gone.** Google suggests adding at least six other structured callouts, such as hours, specialties or payment methods.

What stays the same matters too. Pay-per-lead billing stays. Ads still run only on Search and Maps, in the same spots. Your Google Verified badge carries over.

## Who moves first, and when?

Home services went first. Google's first phase, starting August 2026, covered select U.S. home and storefront businesses in plumbing, HVAC, electrical, appliance repair, house cleaning, lawn care, roofing, pest control and moving.

Late 2026 brings service-area businesses without storefronts and accounts with custom bidding or booking setups. In 2027, Google moves non-U.S. accounts and every remaining category.

Law firms, senior care and other categories not named in the first wave should expect their turn in a later phase. Google hasn't published category-by-category dates. It emails the account administrator 14 days before each account moves, with a reminder 7 days later.

## Why does one target cost per lead matter so much?

Because not every lead is worth the same, and now one number has to cover them all.

A remodeler running bathroom and kitchen leads in one campaign used to be able to value them differently. A firm that takes both family law and estate planning calls faces the same problem. Under the new setup, Google sets a single target for the campaign. If you want separate rules, Google's answer is separate campaigns.

This is the real story of the move. Every step toward automation asks you to trust the platform to know which leads are good. Google can only learn that from what you tell it. If your only feedback is "a lead came in," the system will chase more of whatever is cheapest to produce.

That's why we judge paid channels by [cost per booked appointment, not cost per lead](/insights/cost-per-lead-vs-cost-per-appointment/). The lead count is Google's number. The booked appointment is yours.

## What can you still control?

More than it looks. Google's own description of how Local Services Ads rank puts much of the weight on things you run, not things you bid.

Google's help page on ad rankings lists **responsiveness** to customer inquiries as a factor and says missed calls can hurt it. Profile quality counts too: your rating, your number of reviews, your average response time, good photos and completed verification.

Read that list again. It isn't a list of ad settings. It's a description of how well you answer the phone.

So the lever that survives automation is the oldest one in the business. Pick up. Call back in minutes, not hours. Have a person on the line who can book the next step. Speed-to-lead with a human touch was always good practice. Now it's also part of how your ad gets shown.

## What should you do before your migration email arrives?

Six steps, mostly an afternoon's work.

1. **Save your history.** Screenshot or export your spend, leads and cost per lead by service and by month. Once you migrate, the old reports are gone.
2. **Write down your current setup.** Budgets, bids, service categories, service areas and schedules. You'll want to check them after the move.
3. **Clean up your Google Business Profile.** Name, address, hours and categories now feed your ad directly, and a big change can pause it.
4. **Decide whether to split campaigns.** If two services have very different values, plan separate campaigns before one shared target blurs them.
5. **Add your structured callouts.** At least six, written in plain language a client would care about.
6. **Fix your intake.** Check who answers lead calls, what happens after hours, and how fast messages get a reply. This is the one step that affects your ranking.

After the move, Google says to allow up to two weeks for performance to settle. Watch booked appointments in that window, not just lead volume.

## Is this a reason to depend on Google less?

Yes, and not because Local Services Ads are bad. They still put you at the top of the page for people ready to hire. The concern is concentration.

When most of your new clients come from one platform, that platform's changes become your changes. This one moves your bidding, your budget pacing and your reporting in a single step, and you didn't get a vote.

The hedge is demand you own. A website that answers what your clients actually ask. A Google Business Profile with real reviews. Content that helps AI assistants describe you accurately, because more people now ask ChatGPT or Google's AI answers who to call. We call this Search Essence Optimization. Your clients aren't just searching to find you. They're searching to understand you. When they already understand you before they search, a paid placement becomes one good option among several, not a lifeline.
