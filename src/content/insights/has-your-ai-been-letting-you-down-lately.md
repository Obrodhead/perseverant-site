If ChatGPT, Claude or another assistant has handed you a confident answer that turned out to be wrong, you're in good company. People are leaning on AI for more of their thinking than ever, and many of them are noticing the cracks.

The cause is rarely one bad model. It's a supply problem. AI is only as good as the information it gets. It gets less good information than we assume in two places: inside the conversation, and across the web it learns from.

## Why does AI make things up in the first place?

AI models are built to produce a plausible next word, not to check facts. When they don't know something, the way they're trained often rewards guessing over admitting it.

That isn't a critic's opinion. In September 2025, OpenAI researchers published a paper arguing that standard training and evaluation reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty. Their analogy is a multiple-choice test, where a blind guess can score points and a blank never does. They also explain why some facts are especially easy to get wrong. Patterns like spelling become reliable with enough data. Rare, arbitrary facts, like a specific person's birthday, can't be predicted from patterns at all.

Notice what counts as a rare, arbitrary fact. Your office hours. Which attorney handles estate planning. Whether you serve the next county over. To a language model, the details that matter most about a business are exactly the details it is most likely to guess.

## Reason one: we're giving it less to work with

A short question with no context asks the model to fill every gap with its best guess. "Write a follow-up email to a prospect" gets you something generic. "Write a follow-up to a roofing prospect who got our estimate Tuesday, asked about financing and hasn't replied" gets you something usable. The model didn't get smarter. It got fed.

The habit runs the other way too. The more we trust AI, the less we check it.

- A Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon survey of 319 knowledge workers, presented at the CHI 2025 conference, found that higher confidence in AI was linked to less critical thinking about its output. Higher confidence in one's own skills was linked to more.
- Researchers at BetterUp Labs and Stanford's Social Media Lab, writing in Harvard Business Review in September 2025, coined "workslop" for AI output that looks finished but doesn't move the work forward. In their survey of 1,150 U.S. desk workers, 40% said they had received it in the past month, and each instance took close to two hours to deal with.
- An MIT Media Lab study posted in June 2025 found that people who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed lower brain engagement than people who used search or no tools, and struggled to recall what they had just written. It's an early preprint with 54 participants, and the authors themselves caution against overreading it. Still, it fits a pattern many managers will recognize.

Put those together and you get a loop. We hand AI less context, check its answers less, and pass the results along. A small error in a chat becomes a wrong number in a proposal.

## Reason two: the web it learns from is thinning out

The second supply problem is bigger and slower. AI models learn from enormous amounts of text, much of it from the open web. That web is changing.

Graphite, a marketing agency, sampled 55,400 English-language articles from Common Crawl and ran each through three AI-detection tools. As Axios reported in May 2026, roughly half of new articles published online are now mostly AI-generated. The share climbed quickly after ChatGPT's release and has hovered around half since early 2025.

Why does that matter? In July 2024, researchers from Oxford, Cambridge and other institutions published a study in Nature showing that models trained repeatedly on AI-generated data develop what they called "model collapse." Over generations, the rare, unusual parts of the original data drop away first, and the output narrows toward the average. The authors concluded that data from genuine human interaction becomes more valuable as AI text spreads.

The rare parts that drop away are the specifics. Local details. Niche expertise. The way one particular firm does things. For a business, that's not a side effect. It's the whole story of what makes you worth hiring.

## Is AI actually collapsing into itself?

Not the assistants you use today, and it's worth being precise here. Your chats don't retrain the model in real time. AI labs also filter and curate training data, partly because of this exact risk. Model collapse is a well-documented risk under specific conditions, not a proven description of current chatbots.

There's encouraging research too. In May 2026, a team from King's College London and partner institutions reported in Physical Review Letters that, in simple statistical models, adding even one real data point from outside the system prevented collapse. Those models are far simpler than ChatGPT, and the team plans to test larger systems. The direction is still useful. A little real human signal goes a long way.

So the honest version is not that AI is doomed. It's that AI is starved for specific, verified, human knowledge, and businesses that supply it stand out.

## What this means for your business

When someone asks an AI assistant about you, it answers from whatever it can find. If that material is thin, outdated or inconsistent, it fills the gaps.

Two recent studies from Searchable, a company that sells AI visibility tools, give a sense of scale. Treat them as directional, since the seller ran them. Checking more than 72,000 AI answers about UK high street retailers in July 2026, they found at least one false fact for 64% of businesses, with wrong postcodes the most common error. Smaller retailers fared worst, which the researchers tied to thinner online footprints. An earlier test of more than 13,000 questions about London companies found invented facts for half of small businesses, compared with about a third of large ones.

The pattern is the point. The less a business has published about itself, the more an AI assistant guesses about it.

## The question nobody is answering: if I use AI to write my website, will AI trust my business less?

This is what owners keep asking, usually in some version of "Will Google punish me for using ChatGPT?" Most answers either say "AI content is fine" or "AI content is dangerous" and stop there.

The direct answer is no, AI assistants don't penalize your business for using AI to write. Google said in February 2023 that it rewards helpful, high-quality content however it is produced, judged through its experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust guidelines (often shortened to E-E-A-T). Using AI mainly to game rankings breaks its spam policies. Using it as a writing tool doesn't.

The real risk is sameness. An AI draft is built from what's already common, so it tends to say what every competitor says. "We provide personalized service." "We're committed to excellence." Those lines don't hurt you. They just don't teach anyone, human or machine, anything specific about you. With nothing distinctive to cite, an assistant either guesses or recommends someone who gave it more to work with.

A simple test helps here. If a sentence could appear on a competitor's site unchanged, it isn't helping you get understood. AI can draft the sentence. Only you can supply the facts that make it yours.

## How to give AI better information

### Inside your own chats

- **Give it the situation, not just the task.** Who it's for, what happened, what a good result looks like.
- **Paste the source material.** Notes, the email thread, the policy. The model can't invent what it doesn't have, so it won't need to.
- **Give it permission to say "I don't know."** Ask it to flag anything it isn't sure of rather than fill the gap.
- **Check anything that will leave the building.** Names, numbers, dates and claims, every time.

### For your business online

1. **Publish your facts in plain text.** Services, locations, hours, service areas and who does what, on your own site, in HTML that AI crawlers can read. Some sites hide this behind scripts that many AI crawlers never run. [Here's how to check whether AI assistants can see your website](/insights/why-some-websites-are-invisible-to-ai-assistants/).
2. **Keep every listing identical.** Your website, Google Business Profile and key directories should agree on name, address, phone and services. Conflicting sources produce conflicting answers.
3. **Name your people.** Real bios with credentials and experience give both Google and AI assistants a person to tie expertise to. That's the experience and expertise half of E-E-A-T.
4. **Answer the questions your clients actually ask.** Write from your own experience. The specifics only you know are the specifics AI can't guess.
5. **Ask the assistants what they think of you.** Once a month, ask ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and Google the same few questions a prospect would ask. Save the answers. When something is wrong, fix the source, not the symptom.
